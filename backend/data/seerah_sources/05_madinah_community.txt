Source: Ibn Hisham, Sirat Rasul Allah (from Ibn Ishaq) — The Madinan community and the Constitution of Madinah

In Madinah the Prophet (peace be upon him) found a town of date groves and fort-like compounds, with two main Arab tribes, Aws and Khazraj, who had fought bitter wars, and several Jewish tribes living in and around the oasis, including Banu Qaynuqa, Banu al-Nadir, and Banu Qurayzah. Ending internal Arab fighting and organizing security were immediate needs.

A written pact, often called the Constitution of Madinah (sahifat al-Madinah), is reported from this period. It described the believers as one community (ummah) while also recognizing Jewish groups as communities with their own religion, allied in the defence of the town against outside attack. It assigned responsibility for blood-money and ransom, forbade sheltering criminals against the pact, and placed final decision in disputed matters with Allah and Muhammad. The mosque served as a place of prayer, teaching, consultation, and receiving delegations.

The Muhajirun had left property in Makkah. The Ansar shared their resources. A market independent of older monopolies developed. The call to prayer (adhan) was established after discussion of how to gather people, with Bilal giving the adhan. Hypocrisy (nifaq) appeared among some townsmen who outwardly joined while remaining opposed; Abdullah ibn Ubayy is the most often named figure in that category.

Relations with Jewish tribes were initially framed by the pact. Later conflicts arose over breaches, political rivalry, and incidents that the seerah sources describe in detail tribe by tribe. The Madinan phase therefore combined worship, law, tribal diplomacy, and defence. The Prophet acted as messenger, judge, and military leader of a small state under threat from Quraysh and other enemies. Daily life in this period also included the direction of prayer changing toward the Ka'bah, the fasting of Ramadan being established, and the mosque courtyard serving as a school for companions who had little else. Ibn Hisham presents Madinah less as a sudden empire than as a town whose internal agreements and external raids unfolded year by year after the Hijrah.
