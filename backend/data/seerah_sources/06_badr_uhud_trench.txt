Source: Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum (The Sealed Nectar), Safi-ur-Rahman al-Mubarakpuri — Badr, Uhud, and the Trench

The Battle of Badr took place in Ramadan, 2 AH (624 CE). A Muslim force of just over three hundred met a much larger Quraysh army near the wells of Badr. The Muslims won. Leading opponents such as Abu Jahl were killed. Captives were taken; some were freed after teaching literacy. Badr confirmed that the new community could survive open battle and is described in the Qur'an as a day of furqan (criterion).

The Battle of Uhud followed in 3 AH (625 CE). Quraysh sought revenge. The Prophet (peace be upon him) stationed archers on a hill to protect the rear. After early Muslim success, many archers left their posts to collect spoils. Khalid ibn al-Walid, then fighting for Quraysh, led a cavalry attack through that gap. The Muslim line collapsed. Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib was killed. The Prophet was wounded. Rumours that he had died caused confusion. The Muslims regrouped on the mountain. Uhud was a severe loss and a lesson about discipline and obedience in the seerah accounts.

The Battle of the Trench (al-Khandaq, or al-Ahzab) occurred in 5 AH (627 CE). A coalition including Quraysh and Ghatafan besieged Madinah. On the advice of Salman al-Farisi, the Muslims dug a trench on the exposed side of the town, a tactic unfamiliar to the attackers. The siege lasted weeks. Weather, failed assaults, and negotiations with some confederates weakened the coalition. The siege ended without the city being taken. Afterward, the conflict with Banu Qurayzah is narrated in the classical seerah as a separate, grave episode following alleged treachery during the siege.

These three campaigns — Badr, Uhud, and the Trench — form the core military narrative of the early Madinan years: a small community first victorious, then tested by defeat, then surviving a confederate siege by defensive works and endurance.
