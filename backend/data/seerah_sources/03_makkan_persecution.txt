Source: Ibn Hisham, Sirat Rasul Allah (from Ibn Ishaq) — Persecution in Makkah and the migrations to Abyssinia

As the number of Muslims grew, opposition from Quraysh intensified. Weaker Muslims were tortured to force them to recant. Bilal ibn Rabah, an enslaved Abyssinian, was tortured by Umayyah ibn Khalaf in the heat of the day; Abu Bakr later purchased his freedom. Other early Muslims such as Yasir, Sumayyah, and their son Ammar suffered severe persecution; Sumayyah is remembered as the first martyr among the Muslims.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) himself was protected for years by the standing of his uncle Abu Talib, even though Abu Talib did not accept Islam. Other members of Banu Hashim faced a social and economic boycott. Quraysh leaders mocked the message, demanded miracles on their terms, and pressured families to restrain their Muslim relatives.

Because of this pressure, the Prophet permitted a group of Muslims to migrate to Abyssinia (al-Habashah), whose ruler, the Negus (al-Najashi), was known as a just Christian king. Two migrations to Abyssinia are reported: a smaller first group and a larger second group. Ja'far ibn Abi Talib spoke before the Negus, reciting verses about Mary and Jesus from the Qur'an. The Negus granted protection and refused to hand the migrants back to the Quraysh envoys.

In Makkah the boycott of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib lasted a long period, often described as about three years, during which they were confined to a ravine (shi'b Abi Talib) and cut off from trade and marriage alliances. The boycott eventually collapsed when some Quraysh objected to its injustice and when documents related to it were challenged. These years established a pattern: patient endurance, protection through kinship where it existed, and seeking a safe land when persecution became unbearable. Ibn Hisham's narrative also records public mockery, poets hired against the Muslims, and attempts to bargain: worship of the Makkan gods in exchange for worship of Allah on alternating terms, which the Qur'an rejected. The seerah treats this Makkan stretch as formative: a small community learned to survive without political power, while the later Madinan chapters show what changed once a secure base existed.
